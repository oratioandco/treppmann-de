An AI system that writes measurably more successful fundraising letters

For a donor-funded organization I built an AI system that writes measurably more successful fundraising letters. People bring the input and guidance. The system brings what the analysis of several years of mailings showed.

Organization
Donor-funded non-profit (anonymized)
Starting point
Fundraising letters several times a year. Some raised far more than others, and nobody knew exactly why.
What I did
Analyzed several years of mailings, found the real outliers, checked the patterns against general fundraising practice, and built an AI system that writes the letters with human input and guidance.
Result
Letters from the system are measurably more successful. The very first was already a success.
Three takeaways
Compare fairly before you judge. Only adopt patterns that also hold up against general practice. Build the findings into the system that writes.

The rule in the house: the month decides

Fundraising letters have a season. Some months raise clearly more on average than others. So in the organization, the month was seen as the main reason a letter did well or badly.

But that didn’t explain everything. Some letters raised far more than others, and nobody could say exactly why. And every new letter still started from a blank page.

That isn’t just a problem for fundraising organizations. Wherever a business regularly sends the same kind of text, its old results hold knowledge nobody has analyzed: in quotes, mailings, newsletters, sales letters. And everywhere the temptation is to just tell an AI: write us one of those.

01Compare fairly first

Before you ask what makes a good letter, you need to know which letters really were good. That’s less obvious than it sounds.

First I made several years of results comparable: return per thousand letters sent instead of totals, so a large and a small mailing can sit side by side. Then I took out the season. Each letter was no longer compared with all the others, but with the average of its own month.

02The month sets the baseline. The letter decides.

That flipped the picture. In most months there were letters far above and far below their own average, in the same month. The month explained the level. The text made the difference.

One pile of “good and bad letters” became three groups: letters within the expected range, real outliers above, and real outliers below. Only then was it worth asking what the outliers had in common.

The month sets the baseline. The letter decides.

What the analysis showed

03Only adopt patterns that are more than chance

I put the strong and the weak letters side by side: How do they open? How concrete is the ask? What does the PS do? Add to that the voice analysis and the writing handbook the organization already had.

Then the most important step: I checked every pattern against widely known rules for fundraising letters. Much of it matched, for example that a concrete, tangible ask works better than an abstract mission. Only what passed both checks went into the system: the organization’s own numbers and general expertise. The patterns that only apply to this organization stay with it.

04The analysis becomes a system that writes

From the analysis, the rules, and the writing handbook, I built an AI system on top of Claude, an AI model like ChatGPT. People bring the input and guidance. First the system asks: What’s the occasion? Who is the letter for? Which concrete project should it name? Then it writes the letter step by step and checks the draft against the criteria before moving on.

That’s deliberately more than a “write letter” button. The most common failure of AI writing is a draft that sounds good but misses exactly the specifics only someone in-house knows. The questions collect that knowledge. The checks make sure the findings from the analysis actually reach the letter.

Back to the rule

The month still counts. It sets the baseline you need to judge a letter fairly. But whether a letter turns out well is decided while it’s being written. That’s exactly where the system now works.

06What you can take from this

  1. 1

    Compare fairly before you judge.

    Take out size and season, and measure every result against its own context. Otherwise you mistake the calendar for an insight.

  2. 2

    Only adopt patterns that pass two checks.

    Your own numbers and general expertise. What shows up in only one of the two is often chance.

  3. 3

    Build your findings into the system that writes.

    Let the AI ask and check, not just produce text at the push of a button. The knowledge about the specific occasion sits with your people. A good system collects it before it writes.

When it doesn’t fit: If you only have a handful of comparable mailings. Then the differences are too small to separate real patterns from chance. Start with clear writing rules and collect results.

Something to think about

  • Which texts does your business send regularly whose results you know but have never compared?
  • How would you tell today whether a quote or mailing did well because of the text or because of the timing?
